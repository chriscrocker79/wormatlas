							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>This  term can have multiple meanings, generally concerning the behavior of networks  of groups of animals, cells, cell parts, or molecules. Positive feedback generally acts to increase  the outcome of the original signal, whereas negative feedback acts to decrease  that outcome over time. </p>
									<p>In  neuroscience, feedback can refer to a set of synapses that connect back  directly onto the original neuron in a circuit.</p>
								<p>In  biochemistry, feedback inhibition can refer to circumstances in which the  product of a reaction acts to reduce the activity of the enzyme.</p>

							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>The presence 
								of holes (&#147;windows&#148;) in a membrane or cell process that allow 
								the passage of large molecules, macromolecular structures, or fluids between 
								two sides of the barrier. Some biological structures display natural fenestrations, 
								such as the &#147;pores&#148; in the nuclear membrane, or the holes in the 
								somatic sheath of the gonad (<a href="http://dx.doi.org/10.1006/dbio.1999.9356" target="_blank">Hall 
									et al., 1999</a>). Fenestrations can sometimes be induced experimentally; 
								for instance by laser surgery on the eggshell or the vitelline membrane 
								of the embryo (<a href="http://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00364592" target="_blank">Schierenberg 
								and Junkersdorf, 1992</a><a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00001644;class=Paper" target="_blank"></a>). </td>

							<td valign=top><b><a name="fertilizationmembrane"></a>Fertilization membrane </b></td>
							<td valign=top>Dense vitelline layer (S) </td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>A temporary structure known  to form in some nematode species at the moment of fertilization in order to  physically block subsequent spermatocytes from entering the egg; e.g. blocking  polyspermy. This evanescent structure is apparently  secreted by the oocyte from pre-positioned vesicles and remains in place until  a permanent eggshell can replace it. It  has been seen only after fast freezing fixations in <em>C. elegans</em> (Hall  and Greenstein, unpublished) but has also been noted in <em>Ascaris</em> (<a href="http://dx.doi.org/10.1016/0014-4894(57)90037-1" target="_blank">Fairbairn, 1957</a>; <a href="http://www.jstor.org/discover/10.2307/3276689" target="_blank">Foor, 1967</a>; <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/query.fcgi?db=pubmed&cmd=Retrieve&dopt=AbstractPlus&list_uids=769504&query_hl=17&itool=pubmed_docsum" target="_blank">Anya 1976</a>) .</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="gglossary.htm#glycocalyx" target="_blank">Glycocalyx</a></p></td>

								stain</a></b></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>A specific 
								stain for nuclear DNA which labels all cell nuclei and can be viewed by 
								light microscopy. It contains <a href="http://stainsfile.info/StainsFile/dyes/42685.htm" target="_blank">fuchsin</a>, a colorless dye which has a strong 
								affinity for DNA and produces red color in its presence. A fixed animal 
								can be stained with Feulgen to highlight the DNA content of all nuclei. 
								Larval stages give the most dramatic results (<a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/postemblin_1977/toc.html" target="_blank">Sulston 
									and Horvitz, 1977</a>).</td>
						</tr>

							<td valign=top><b><a name="fiber" id="fiber"></a>Fiber</b></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>Term used to refer to various long thin extended  objects. It can refer to a subcellular  structure such as a thick filament of the internal  cytoskeleton, or to an extracellular object lying in the basal lamina or cuticle  layers. More often the term has been  used to refer to either a cellular process (for instance a &ldquo;nerve fiber&rdquo; or  &ldquo;muscle fiber&rdquo;) or to a structure made up from a group of cell  processes, or even a whole groups of cells.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><b><a name="fiberlayers" id="fiberlayers"></a><a href="../hermaphrodite/cuticle/Images/cutfig6leg.htm" target="_blank">Fiber 
								layers</a></b></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>Three (or possibly just two) 
								of the innermost layers of the cuticle (corresponding to layers 6, 7, 
								8 of <i>Ascaris</i> cuticle) which display tightly woven fibers that run 
								coherently in helical or circumferential orientations around the body. 
								Two of these layers appear to run at 65&#176; angle to the longitudinal 
								body axis, but in opposite directions to each other (<a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK20029/" target="_blank">Kramer, 1997</a>). These layers lie between the boundary zone and the basal 
								layer. Together they comprise the<a href="../hermaphrodite/cuticle/Images/cutfig3leg.htm" target="_blank"> basal zone (Bs)</a> of the cuticle (<a href="http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/book/9780120996513" target="_blank">Bird 
									and Bird, 1991</a>). </p>
									<p>Some roller and <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_cuticle/cuticlefig3.jpg" target="_blank">dumpy mutations</a> of collagen genes 
										may disrupt this helical organization, causing the entire animal to adopt 
										a helical twist to the outer cuticle and forcing the animal to <a href="http://www.wormclassroom.org/files/worm/RolHalf.mov" target="_blank">move helically 
											in a rolling motion</a>, rather than in a <a href="http://labs.bio.unc.edu/Goldstein/crawl.mov" target="_blank">sinusoidal path</a>. The fiber layers 
										can show visible lamination between themselves, and may be visualized 
										as striated bands by various imaging modalities, including polarized light, 
									    <a href="../hermaphrodite/cuticle/Images/cutfig4leg.htm" target="_blank">TEM</a>, and<a href="../hermaphrodite/cuticle/Images/cutfig4leg.htm" target="_blank"> freeze etching</a>.</p>
								<p><br>
									See <a href="bglossary.htm#basallayer" target="_blank">Basal 
										layer</a><br>
									See <a href="bglossary.htm#basalzone" target="_blank">Basal 
										zone</a><br>

										zone</a><br>
									See <a href="cglossary.htm#corticallayer" target="_blank">Cortical

									See <a href="rglossary.htm#roller" target="_blank">Roller</a><br>
							See <a href="lglossary.htm#locomotion" target="_blank">Locomotion</a></p></td>

							<td valign=top><b><a name="fibril"></a>Fibril</b></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>This term may refer to any  of a number of long thin objects, just as the word &ldquo;fiber&rdquo; has been used rather  widely. Fibrils (as opposed to &ldquo;fibers&rdquo;) have most often been identified with a  class of extracellular macromolecules (perhaps extended collagens) lying within  the medial zone of the cuticle.&nbsp; When clustered macromolecules lie in register  side by side, the resulting fibrils may present a distinctive striated  appearance.</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="#fiber">Fiber </a><br>
										See <a href="#fibrillayer">Fibril layer </a><br>
								</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><b><a name="fibrillayer" id="fibrillayer"></a><a href="../hermaphrodite/cuticle/Images/cutfig3leg.htm" target="_blank">Fibril 
								layer</a></b></td>
							<td valign=top>Fibrillar 
								layer(S)</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>A portion of the medial zone 
								of the cuticle, lying just beneath the cortical zone and above the matrix 
								layer. This thin layer contains a more densely arrayed set of fibrils

							<td valign=top><p>A<a href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig11leg.htm" target="_blank"> complex structure</a> located in hypodermal cells underlying the bodywall muscle that generally includes two hemi-adherens 
								junctions (or hemidesmosomes) facing outward from the organelle and is responsible for securing these

							<td valign=top><b><a name="fourdimensionalmicroscopy"></a><a href="http://www.loci.wisc.edu/4d/" target="_blank">Four-dimensional microscopy </a></b></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Four-dimensional imaging (S)<br>
								4D video recording (S) </p></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>The generation of time-lapse recordings of multiple image planes (X,Y coordinates) at different focal depths (Z coordinate). This technique is particularly useful for examination of the early development of the <em>C. elegans </em>embryo. These recordings can be used to create a &ldquo;movie&rdquo;  to follow cell divisions and  movements over time.</td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><b><a name="fourfoldstage"></a><a href="http://worms.zoology.wisc.edu/morphogenesis.jpg" target="_blank">Four-fold 
								stage</a></b></td>
							<td valign=top>Late 
								pretzel stage (S)</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>The final stage of embryogenesis 
								prior to hatching, where the embryonic worm is bent in three places within 
								the eggshell.</p>
									<p><br>
										See<a href="pglossary.htm#pretzelstage" target="_blank"> Pretzel stage</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><b><a name="freeliving"></a>Free 
								living</b></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>Describes 
								the life style of an animal that is not living as a parasite within the 
								body of a host animal or plant. <i>C. elegans</i> is a free living soil 
								nematode.</td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><b><a name="freezecracking"></a>Freeze-cracking</b></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>A method of fixation used for 
								preparing animals for immunofluorescent staining in which the whole nematode 
								is exposed to repeated cycles of freezing and thawing in dry ice/ethanol 
								mixture prior to aldehyde fixation. The repeated cycle acts to break open 
								the tough cuticle exterior to permeabilize the internal tissues to fixative 
								and antibodies.</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/EMmethods/Freezecrack.htm" target="_blank">Anatomical 
											Methods-Freeze-crack and Staining Protocol</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><b><a name="fusion"></a>Fusion</b></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p><strong>1)</strong> Process through which two 
								cells join to make one structure. In <i>C. elegans</i>, many cells in 
								the developing nematode eventually undergo fusion to form syncytial tissues 
								(e.g. hypodermis, muscle, glands) (<a href="http://dx.doi.org/10.1006/dbio.1994.1041" target="_blank">Podbilewicz 
									and White, 1994</a>). Cell fusion is also a key step in fertilization, bringing the spermatozoon 
						  inside of the oocyte (fusion of gametes).</p>
									<p><strong>2)</strong> Alternately, some cells 
										can undergo self-fusion in order to produce ring-like structures; these 
										include the sheath cells of many sensilla and some processes in gland 
										cells.</p>
								<p><strong>3)</strong> Membrane fusion is involved 
									during the release of chemical transmitters from synaptic vesicles or 
									release of other intercellular signals from cells or glandular secretions.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><b><a name="fusionpore"></a>Fusion 
								pore</b></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>An intermediate 
								structure thought to occur during the process of membrane fusion between 
								two cells, or membrane-bound organelles. Such structures are generally not 
						  well demonstrated by TEM as yet in <i>C. elegans</i> (but see <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper0http://dx.doi.org/10.1006/dbio.1998.91730003418;class=Paper" target="_blank">Nguyen et al., 1999</a>), although cell fusions are commonplace.</p>
									<p>Alternately, a distinctive type of <a href="../hermaphrodite/germ line/Images/germfig9leg.htm" target="_blank">fusion pore has been described in the 
										spermatid</a>, in which a membranous organelle within the cell fuses with the 
										plasma membrane (<a href="http://jcs.biologists.org/content/110/9/1073.long" target="_blank">Achanzar 
											and Ward, 1997</a>). Here the fusion pore is reinforced by an electron 
										dense &#147;collar&#148; on its cytoplasmic face that holds the pore open 
									  to allow the contents of the membranous organelle to flow through.</p>
								<p>A <a href="../hermaphrodite/germ line/Images/germfig2leg.htm" target="_blank">persistent fusion pore</a>, albeit much larger, is maintained between germ 
									cells and the rachis in the syncytial gonad; here again the pore is apparently 
									maintained in an open state, reinforced by an electron dense material on 
									the cytoplasmic face, most likely consisting of hemicentin (<a href="http://dev.biologists.org/content/128/6/883.long" target="_blank">Vogel 
							and Hedgecock, 2001</a>; Hall, unpublished).</p></td>
						</tr>
