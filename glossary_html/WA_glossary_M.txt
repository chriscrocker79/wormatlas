							<td valign=top><p>A postulated mechanism for  the initiation of 
								cell death, in which one cell dies as a result of an external  signal 
								from another &quot;killer cell&quot;, or dies due to phagocytosis by 
								a neighboring cell while  still healthy (<a 
href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00000463;class=Paper" 
target="_blank">Sulston and White, 1980</a>; <a 
href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00001419;class=Paper" 
target="_blank">White et al., 1991</a>).&nbsp;</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a 
href="eglossary.htm#engulfment" target="_blank">Engulfment</a> <br>
										See <a 
href="kglossary.htm#killercell" 
target="_blank">Killer cell</a><br>
										See <a href="sglossary.htm#suicide" target="_blank">Suicide</a><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><br>
										</font></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="muscle"></a><a href="../hermaphrodite/muscleintro/MusIntroframeset.html" target="_blank">Muscle</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Somatic cell(s) whose principal function is to 
								provide contractile forces, either to modify body posture or to 
								control the  position or motions of particular tissues or organs. In 
								the nematode, which lacks a skeleton, most  muscles are anchored to 
								the cuticle. The body&rsquo;s high turgor pressure provides  rigidity 
								to the cuticle, which can then respond to muscle contractions to 
								modify the body shape. </p>
									<p>Some muscle cells  in <em>C. elegans </em>act alone, but the  principal muscles of the <a href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/MusSomaticframeset.html" target="_blank">bodywall</a> and<a href="../hermaphrodite/pharynx/Phaframeset.html" target="_blank"> pharynx</a> operate in groups, either in  synchrony, in sequence, or in 
										opposition to provide coordinated, smoothly  controlled motions. 
										Individual muscle cells are often small in size, spindle  shaped, and 
										consist of single sarcomeres or obliquely striated sarcomeres (as  in 
							      bodywall muscle). </p>
								<p>Several cell types have <a href="../hermaphrodite/musclenonstriated/MusNonstriframeset.html" target="_blank">smooth muscle</a> properties, 
									including the gonad sheath, the uterine muscles, and the anal 
									sphincter  muscle. Some muscle types are sex-specific,  including the <a href="../hermaphrodite/egglaying apparatus/jump.html?newLink=mainframe.htm&newAnchor=Uterineandvulvalmuscles4" target="_blank">vulval and uterine muscles</a> of the hermaphrodite, 
		              and the large  variety of <a href="../male/musclemale/Musmaleframeset.html" target="_blank">specialized muscles in the male tail</a>.</p>
								<p><br>
									See <a href="bglossary.htm#bodywallmuscle" 
target="_blank">Bodywall muscle</a><br>
									See <a href="dglossary.htm#diagonalmuscle" 
target="_blank">Diagonal muscle</a><br>
									See <a href="dglossary.htm#dilatormuscle" 
target="_blank">Dilator muscle</a><br>
									See <a href="eglossary.htm#erectormuscle" 
target="_blank">Erector muscle</a><br>
									See <a href="eglossary.htm#extensormuscle" 
target="_blank">Extensor muscle</a><br>
									See <a href="#myoepithelium">Myoepithelium </a><br>
									See <a href="oglossary.htm#obliquemuscle" 
target="_blank">Oblique muscle</a><br>
									See <a href="pglossary.htm#pharyngealmuscle" 
target="_blank">Pharyngeal muscle</a><br>
									See <a href="rglossary.htm#retractormuscle" target="_blank">Retractor muscle</a><br>
									See <a href="sglossary.htm#sphinctermuscle" target="_blank">Sphincter muscle</a><br>
									See <a href="uglossary.htm#uterinemuscle" target="_blank">Uterine muscle</a><br>
									See <a href="#Mline">M line</a><br>
									See <a href="zglossary.htm#zdisc" target="_blank">Z disc</a><br>
									See <a href="aglossary.htm#actin" 
target="_blank">Actin</a><br>
									See <a href="#myosin">Myosin</a><br>
									See <a href="tglossary.htm#titin" target="_blank">Titin</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a 
name="musclearm"></a><a 
href="../hermaphrodite/muscleintro/jump.html?newLink=mainframe.htm&newAnchor=Musclearms4" 
target="_blank">Muscle arm</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>Innervation process  (S) <br></td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A process extension from the  muscle belly by 
								which the muscle cells reach to the nerve cords to obtain 
								innervation. This extended muscle  process is a common feature 
								of practically all somatic muscles, but is not  found in the pharynx, 
								where neurons extend processes to the muscle cell  directly. The 
								typical bodywall muscle has 3 to 5 muscle arms. </p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="#Musclebelly">Muscle belly</a><br>
										See <a href="#Muscleplate">Muscle plate</a><br>
										See <a 
href="pglossary.htm#plexus" 
target="_blank">Plexus</a><br>
										See <a href="sglossary.htm#spur" target="_blank">Spur </a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a 
name="Musclebelly"></a><a 
href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig10leg.htm" 
target="_blank">Muscle belly </a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>Sarcoplasm (S) </td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>The cytoplasmic swelling  surrounding  the 
								muscle cell nucleus, which contains many mitochondria and  ribosomes, 
								but excludes that portion of the cell devoted to the  sarcomeres. 
								Muscle cells are mostly  spindle-shaped, with sarcomeres extending to 
								the distal, thinner portions of  the spindle, while the nucleus lies 
								within the fatter muscle belly.</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="#musclearm">Muscle arm </a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="muslcefiber"></a>Muscle fiber </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>That portion of the muscle  quadrant that 
								contains the <a 
href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig9leg.htm" 
target="_blank">myofilament lattice</a>. This term is not often 
								used in nematode literature. It might refer to the myofilament 
								lattice  within a since muscle cell, or to the <a 
href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig10leg.htm" 
target="_blank">collection of sarcomeres</a> within a single muscle 
				    cell.</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="sglossary.htm#sarcomere" target="_blank">Sarcomere</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a 
name="Muscleplate"></a><a 
href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/Ware_build0.2/art/fig26_large.jpg" 
target="_blank">Muscle plate</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>Motor end plate (S)<b></b> <br>
								Plexus (S) </td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Specialized  interdigitating ends of the muscle 
								arms form a localized plexus along certain  portions of the 
								longitudinal nerve cords and nerve ring. This plexus, known as the 
								&ldquo;muscle plate&rdquo;, is  the site of muscle innervation by the 
								motorneurons. This portion of each muscle arm tends to be 
								very thin and electron lucent, lacking any cytoplasmic organelles. 
								The chemical synapses formed by the motor  axons are called 
								neuromuscular junctions (NMJs). </p>
									<p>Some gap junctions (GJs) may  also 
										occur here between nerve and muscle or between nearby muscle arms. 
										The most distinctive muscle  plates form just underneath the nerve 
										ring (forming a complete cylinder), and  opposite the principal motor 
										neuron axons of the ventral and dorsal nerve cords  (as lengthwise 
										bands) (<a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/Ware_build0.2/toc.html" 
target="_blank">Ware et al., 1975</a>).</p>
								<p><br>
									See <a href="gglossary.htm#gapjunction" target="_blank">Gap junction</a> <br>
									See <a 
href="nglossary.htm#neuromuscularjunction" 
target="_blank">Neuromuscular 
										junction</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="musclequadrant"></a><a 
href="../hermaphrodite/muscleintro/Images/musfig3leg.htm" 
target="_blank">Muscle quadrant </a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p><a href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig7leg.htm" target="_blank">Longitudinal bodywall muscles</a> are grouped into 
								four groups known as the <a 
href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig10leg.htm" 
target="_blank">muscle quadrants</a>. Each quadrant comprises a 
								double row of  elongated muscle cells that lie very close together, 
								covered by a common basal  lamina which separates them from 
								neighboring territories of hypodermis and  nerves in the bodywall, 
							and from the pseudocoelomic fluid more centrally.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="musclestabilization"></a>Muscle stabilization </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Structures and/or developmental events by which 
								the body muscles are anchored  to the bodywall in order to provide 
								strong resistance to the forces of muscle  contraction, so that 
								muscle contraction causes motion of the whole body rather  than 
								damage to the sarcomere.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="mutagenesis"></a>Mutagenesis/ Mutagenized </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A procedure used to increase  the rate of 
								genetic mutations within a population of animals. In <em>C. 
									elegans</em>, common mutagenic agents  include ethylmethane sulfonate 
								(EMS), X-rays,  and transposon (Tc1) insertion. </p>
									<p>Mutagenesis has been 
										used to create large libraries of nematodes harboring deletions in 
										their DNA. Screening these libraries has resulted in the isolation of 
										lines carrying deletions in specific genes. Deletion strains produced 
										by <a href="http://celeganskoconsortium.omrf.org/" 
target="_blank">The <em>C. elegans</em> Knockout Consortium</a> are 
										freely available to researchers upon request. </p>
								<p><br>
									See <a href="eglossary.htm#ems" target="_blank">EMS</a><br>
									See<a href="rglossary.htm#rnai" target="_blank"> RNAi</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="mutualism"></a>Mutualism</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Describes  a lifestyle in which two species 
								live together, to the benefit of both  organisms. In many cases of 
								mutualism,  the relationship is obligate, and neither species can 
								thrive apart from the  other. If only one species derives  obvious 
								benefits, it is generally considered commensalism. It has been 
								suggested that <em>C. elegans</em> may have a commensal relationship 
								with pillbugs in  the wild, living under the shell of the pillbug and 
								perhaps consuming bacteria  there (<a 
href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00001874;class=Paper" 
target="_blank">Baird et al., 1994</a>).</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="cglossary.htm#commensal" 
target="_blank">Commensal</a><br>
										See <a href="fglossary.htm#freeliving" 
target="_blank">Free-living</a><br>
										See <a href="pglossary.htm#parasite" 
target="_blank">Parasite</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="myoblast"></a>Myoblast</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Stem cell that divides to  produce muscle cells.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a 
name="myoepithelium"></a>Myoepithelial/ Myoepithelium</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Describing any tissue that  shares properties 
								of both muscle and of epithelial tissue. Some single sarcomere 
								muscles, such as  pharyngeal muscle cells, have this character since 
								they secrete cuticle for the  pharyngeal lumen, and cuticle secretion 
								is generally considered an epithelial  function. Some smooth muscle 
								tissues, such as the somatic sheath of the gonad, also fit this 
								definition, as they  completely enclose the germline cells.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="myofilament"></a>Myofilament</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>Myofiber (S) <b></b>Myofibril (S) </td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A generic name referring to  both the <a 
href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig12leg.htm" 
target="_blank">thick and thin filaments</a> (<a 
href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=mboc4.figgrp.3043" 
target="_blank">myosin</a> and <a 
href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=mboc4.figgrp.2969" 
target="_blank">actin</a> respectively). They make up the &ldquo;<a 
href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig9leg.htm" 
target="_blank">myofilament lattice</a>&rdquo;, a  well organized 
								network of parallel filaments that are intermingled to form the <a 
href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig10leg.htm" 
target="_blank">sarcomere</a> in &ldquo;obliquely striated&rdquo; and 
								&ldquo;single sarcomere&rdquo; muscles, but can also  lie in more d
							ispersed, disorganized arrays within smooth muscles.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="myofilamentlattice"></a><a 
href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/handbook/fig.s/musfig34c.jpg" 
target="_blank">Myofilament lattice</a> </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>The organized set of <a 
href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=cooper.figgrp.1797" 
target="_blank">overlapping thick and thin filaments</a> (<a 
href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=mboc4.figgrp.3043" 
target="_blank">myosin</a> and <a 
href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=mboc4.figgrp.2969" 
target="_blank">actin</a> respectively) that  together compose the 
								dominant feature of the muscle <a 
href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig10leg.htm" 
target="_blank">sarcomere</a>, anchored on each  end to the <a 
href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig13leg.htm" 
target="_blank">dense bodies</a>, or Z bands. Each thick 
								filament is surrounded by
								evenly spaced thin filaments, all  lying in parallel; they are 
								believed to slide past one another during muscle  contraction. Thin 
								filaments outnumber  thick filaments by a ratio of 5:1 in mature 
				    bodywall muscles. </p>
									<p>Orderly development of the lattice can be 
										monitored under polarized light by light microscopy, or in thin 
										sections by  electron microscopy, and is disrupted in many 
										mutants.</p>
								<p><br>
									See <a href="#myofilament">Myofilament</a><br>
									See <a href="oglossary.htm#obliquelystriated" 
target="_blank">Obliquely striated</a><br>
									See <a href="sglossary.htm#sarcomere" target="_blank">Sarcomere</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="myogenesis"></a><a href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/jump.html?newLink=mainframe.htm&newAnchor=Developmentofsomaticmuscle3" target="_blank">Myogenesis</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>Muscle development (S) </td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>The set of developmental steps involved in 
								muscle  differentiation and growth, including the <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bookshelf/br.fcgi?book=wormbook&part=sarcomereassemblycelegansmusc&rendertype=figure&id=A2686" target="_blank">gradual 
								construction of the early  sarcomere</a>, the mature sarcomere, and the 
							increase in sarcomere numbers per cell.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="myogeniccontroloflocomotion" id="myogeniccontroloflocomotion"></a>Myogenic control of locomotion </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A theory that the basic pattern of locomotion in nematodes  is patterned by the direct interconnections between muscle cells (via muscle  arm gap junctions) and not due to the pattern of synaptic inputs from motor  neurons (<a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/entrez?db=pubmed&cmd=Retrieve&dopt=AbstractPlus&list_uids=5139022&query_hl=3&itool=pubmed_docsum" target="_blank">Crofton, 1971</a>).</p>
									<p>See <a href="nglossary.htm#neurocraticcontroloflocomotion" target="_blank">Neurocratic control of  locomotion </a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="myosin"></a><a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=mboc4.figgrp.3043" target="_blank">Myosin</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>Thick filament (S) </td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Very large elongated  macromolecular protein 
								that forms the<a 
href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=ce2.figgrp.533" 
target="_blank"> major cytoskeletal element</a> within muscle  cells, <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=ce2.figgrp.547" 
target="_blank">the thick filament</a>. Two isoforms of myosin heavy 
								chain, A and B, are  localized to different regions of individual 
								thick filaments in bodywall muscle  (<a 
href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00000647;class=Paper" 
target="_blank">Miller et al., 1983</a>), binding to a paramyosin (<a 
href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/query.fcgi?cmd=Search&db=books&doptcmdl=GenBookHL&term=paramyosin+AND+ce2%5Bbook%5D+AND+1478%5Buid%5D&rid=ce2.section.532#542" 
target="_blank
  ">Moerman and Fire, 1997</a>) core protein to form a long  straight 
								filament with myosin motors oriented in opposite directions at each 
								end of the thick filament. Each myosin  heavy chain contains a motor 
								domain that translocates myosin relative to  adjacent actin thin 
								filaments to provide the force of muscle contraction.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="myosinrod"></a><a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=mboc4.figgrp.3043" target="_blank">Myosin rod</a> </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>The central  portion of the myosin heavy chain 
								molecule, a coiled-coil domain which is&nbsp; long and straight, with 
								a globular motor  domain on one end and a non-helical tailpiece on 
								the other end (<a 
href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00001394;class=Paper" 
target="_blank">Epstein, 1990</a>; <a 
href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00004378;class=Paper" 
target="_blank">Hoppe and Waterston, 2000</a>).</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="myosintailpiece"></a>Myosin tailpiece </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>The  non-helical portion of the myosin heavy 
								chain protein, which lies at the  opposite end of the myosin rod from

						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="myotactin"></a><a 
href="http://www.jcb.org/cgi/content/full/146/3/659/F1" 
target="_blank">Myotactin</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Membrane protein involved in  the assembly and 
								positioning of muscle/hypodermal <a href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig11leg.htm" target="_blank">fibrous organelles</a> (hemi-adherens 
		      junctions) (<a
