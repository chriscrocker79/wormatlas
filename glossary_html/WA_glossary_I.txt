						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="immuneresponse"></a>Immune response</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif">Innate immunity   
								(S)</font></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>The mechanism of resisting pathogen infection. Because of the chemical and mechanical nature of the nematode's cuticle, most pathogens infect <em>C. elegans</em> after being ingested. As such, <em>C. elegans</em> has a navigation system that allows it to try and avoid noxious factors. Pathogen avoidance may be mediated by the <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_signalingimmuneresponse/signalingimmuneresponse.html#d0e637" target="_blank">Toll receptor</a> (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00004692;class=Paper" target="_blank">Pujol et al., 2001</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_signalingimmuneresponse/signalingimmuneresponse.html" target="_blank">Ewbank, 2006</a>). Other work has implicated a role for <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_signalingimmuneresponse/signalingimmuneresponse.html#d0e986" target="_blank">stress response</a> genes in the immune response (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00005917;class=Paper" target="_blank">Garsin et al., 2003</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_signalingimmuneresponse/signalingimmuneresponse.html" target="_blank">Ewbank, 2006</a>). See <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00025180;class=Paper" target="_blank">Gravato-Nobre and Hodgkin (2005)</a> for review on innate immunity in <em>C. elegans</em>. </td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><b><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="imprinting"></a>Imprinting</font></b></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A simple form of learning in nematodes in which an individual animal's exposure to a specific set of conditions, such as thermal (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00000161;class=Paper" target="_blank">Hedgecock and Russell, 1975</a>) and olfactory cues (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00026662;class=Paper" target="_blank">Remy and Hobert, 2005</a>), during a critical developmental time period alters the animal's behavior upon encountering similar conditions later in life. Olfactory imprinting was found to be mediated by a single pair of interneurons suggesting a role for chemosensory receptors in this behavior (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00026662;class=Paper" target="_blank">Remy and Hobert, 2005</a>).<br>
							</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><b><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="inbred"></a>Inbred/ Inbreeding </font></b></td>
							<td valign=top><p>&nbsp;</p></td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>The repeated crossing of a strain back  into itself or with a closely related strain so that the resulting progeny tend  to become highly homozygous at most genetic loci.</p>
									<p><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><br>
										See <a href="oglossary.htm#outcross" target="_blank">Outcross </a><br>
										See <a href="bglossary.htm#backcross" target="_blank">Backcross</a> </font></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><b><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="inbreedingdepression"></a>Inbreeding depression </font></b></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A reduction in fecundity associated with  repeated generations of inbreeding. This  phenomenon is well known for many animal species, including some bisexual  nematodes (Nigon, 1949), but is not likely to be as large a factor in the self-fertilizing hermaphrodite though it has been indicated in the determination of the sex ratio of <em>C. elegans</em> populations (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00005921;class=Paper" target="_blank">Cutter et al., 2003</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00005186;class=Paper" target="_blank">Stewart and Phillips, 2002</a>).</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="indirectdevelopment"></a>Indirect development </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"></font>Heterogonic (S) </td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>Describes a lifestyle in  which a parasitic species must undergo one full generation as a free-living  species. The free-living animals proceed  through sexual reproduction before undergoing developmental arrest at an  intermediate larval stage. The arrested larvae must find a host and infect it  before resuming development, or die. The  infective animals may reproduce through parthenogenesis as females inside the host (<a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/query.fcgi?db=pubmed&cmd=Retrieve&dopt=Abstract&list_uids=1713962&query_hl=9&itool=pubmed_docsum" target="_blank">Hawdon and Schad, 1991</a>).</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><a name="inductivesignalling"></a>Inductive signaling </strong></td>
							<td valign=top>Induction (S)</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A mechanism of tissue  differentiation where a signal or signals produced extrinsic to the cell or  tissue is received at the cell membrane and cause the cell to alter its status.  It has been demonstrated in <em>C. elegans</em> in some key developmental steps, such as in <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_notchembryo/notchembryo.html" target="_blank">early embryogenesis</a>, postembryonic <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_vulvaldev/vulvaldev.html" target="_blank">development of the vulva</a>, and in <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_maledevelopment/maledevelopment.html" target="_blank">the connection of  the vas deferens to the cloaca</a> (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00000463;class=Paper" target="_blank">Sulston and White, 1980</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00000193;class=Paper" target="_blank">Kimble and Hirsh,  1979</a>). There is generally no lineal  relationship between the inducing cell and those responding to the inducing  signal.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="infection"></a>Infection</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"></font></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td align="left" valign=top><p>The growth of a secondary organism within  the cells or body cavity of a host animal.&nbsp;<em>C. elegans</em> has been shown to be subject to infection by bacteria and yeasts (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00003376;class=Paper" target="_blank">Tan et al., 1999</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00004466;class=Paper" target="_blank">Labrousse et al., 2000</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00005119;class=Paper" target="_blank">Aballay and Ausubel, 2002</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00005590;class=Paper" target="_blank">Mylonakis et al., 2002</a>; Hall, unpublished) though not from viruses, possibly due to the RNAi surveillance system which could provide virus defense (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00024258;class=Paper" target="_blank">Vastenhouw and Plasterk, 2001</a>). In some  cases, infection seems to lead to death of the animal either by toxic interactions  or by blocking feeding. Other infections  may interfere with sexual reproduction by blocking normal use of the male tail  apparatus.</p>
									<p>There is no evidence to our  knowledge that <em>C. elegans</em> itself can live as a parasite, but may be free-living  and/or live commensally with pillbugs, as has been shown for several  related rhabditid species (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00001874;class=Paper" target="_blank">Baird et al., 1994</a>).</p>
								<p><br>
									See <a href="cglossary.htm#commensal" target="_blank">Commensal</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><b><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="infertile"></a>Infertile</font></b></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"></font>Sterile (S) </td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>

									<p>&nbsp; </p></td>

							<td valign=top>Finger-like cell extensions  which precede cell invasion, as is seen on the anchor cell as it invades the  vulval epithelium <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00005989;class=Paper" target="_blank">(Sherwood and Sternberg, 2003</a>).</td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="invagination"></a>Invagination</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><p><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a href="/neurons.htm/aia.htm" target="_blank"></a> </font>Ingression (S) </p>
									<p>&nbsp; </p></td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>This is a process by which there is movement or folding inward. Common processes in <em>C. elegans</em> where invagination is seen include:</p>
									<p><strong>1)</strong> The formation of a <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bookshelf/br.fcgi?book=wormbook&part=celldivision&rendertype=figure&id=A2131" target="_blank">cleavage furrow</a> during cell division. </p>
								<p><strong>2)</strong> The initial step of <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bookshelf/br.fcgi?book=wormbook&part=gastrulation&rendertype=figure&id=A4549" target="_blank">gastrulation</a> during embryonic development where cells move and fold to create different tissue layers.</p>
								<p><strong>3)</strong> A step during <a href="../hermaphrodite/egglaying apparatus/Images/eggfig8leg.htm?book=wormbook&part=vulvaldev&rendertype=figure&id=A3451" target="_blank">vulval morphogenesis</a> in which cells detach from the cuticle allowing for the vulval sheet to bend inward. This step establishes the beginning of the vulval lumen. </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="invasion"></a>Invasion</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><p><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a href="/neurons.htm/aia.htm" target="_blank"></a> </font>Cell invasion </p>
									<p>&nbsp; </p></td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A biological mechanism  through which a cell can cross a basement membrane to penetrate into a  neighboring tissue &ndash; an important feature in both metastasis (intravasation,  etc.) (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00027196;class=Paper" target="_blank">Sherwood, 2006</a>) and intercellular tube formation (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00005485;class=Paper" target="_blank">Buechner, 2002</a>). In <em>C. elegans</em>, cell invasion is known to  occur during formation of the vulval uterine junction, when the anchor cell  crosses the basal laminae of the uterus and of the vulval epithelium to link  these tissues (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00002540;class=Paper" target="_blank">Newman and Sternberg, 1996</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00005989;class=Paper" target="_blank">Sherwood and Sternberg, 2003</a>). Mutations that weaken the basal lamina can  also cause aberrant cell invasions (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00006014;class=Paper" target="_blank">Huang et al., 2003</a>). Long distance migration and morphogenesis of  embryonic and larval cells (sex myoblast, anchor cell) may also involve invasion  events. Conversely, in parasitic species, this term can refer to the event by which a nematode enters the tissues of its host species (plant, animal or even another nematode). </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="inverseembryo"></a>Inverse embryo </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top>Mirror image embryo </td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="ipsilateral"></a>Ipsilateral</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif">Contralateral (A) </font></td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top>A cell or cell extension that lies on the same side of the midline as a reference object. </td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="isothermaltracking"></a>Isothermal tracking </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>See <a href="tglossary.htm#thermotaxis" target="_blank">Thermotaxis</a> </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="isthmus"></a><a href="../hermaphrodite/pharynx/Images/phafig2leg.htm?rid=wormbook.figgrp.13650" target="_blank">Isthmus</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a href="/neurons.htm/aiy.htm" target="_blank"></a> </font></td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A narrowing of the pharynx between the first and second bulbs (between metacorpus and postcorpus) around  which the nerve ring forms.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="iteroparous"></a>Iteroparous/ iteroparity </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a href="/neurons.htm/aiy.htm" target="_blank"></a> Semelparous/ semelparity (converse) </font></td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>The animal&rsquo;s  ability to produce young at repeated intervals over its lifespan rather than  just once during during a narrow time period (as in &ldquo;semelparous&rdquo;  animals). Although its lifespan is quite  brief, <em>C. elegans</em> should probably be  considered to be iteroparous.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><b><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="ivermectin"></a><a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivermectin" target="_blank">Ivermectin</a></font></b></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a href="/neurons.htm/aiy.htm" target="_blank"></a> </font></td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A drug commonly used in humans and animals as an anti-parasite medication especially in the treatment of intestinal worms. <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_ionotropicglutatmaterecep/ionotropicglutatmaterecep.html#sec2" target="_blank">Research in <em>C. elegans</em></a> has shows that this drug activates the glutamate-gated chloride channels (<a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_ionotropicglutatmaterecep/ionotropicglutatmaterecep.html" target="_blank">Brockie and Maricq, 2006</a>) that has the effect of inhibiting the activity of excitable cells such as muscles and neurons which express these channels. Genetic (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00003954;class=Paper" target="_blank">Dent et al., 2000</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00005862;class=Paper" target="_blank">Keane and Avery, 2003</a>) and pharmacological approaches (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00004645;class=Paper" target="_blank">Pemberton et al., 2001</a>) in <em>C. elegans</em> have helped to identify genes and how ivermectin functions to activate these channels. </p></td>
						</tr>
