							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="rselection"></a><a href="http://www.bio.miami.edu/tom/courses/bil160/bil160goods/16_rKselection.html" target="_blank">r-selection</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><p>&nbsp;</p></td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style4"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Evolutionary survival strategy which is typically found in unstable environments where the ability to reproduce quickly is critical for successful survival. r is the growth rate term in the <a href="http://mathworld.wolfram.com/LogisticEquation.html" target="_blank">logistic equation (Verhulst model) </a> of population dynamics. Traits that correspond to species that follow an r-selected behavioral strategy usually include: small size, rapid development, high rates of fertility, short generation time and  relatively brief life span. </p>
									<p>Free-living <em>C. elegans</em> adopts this  strategy to make best use of its uncertain environmental conditions. K-selection strategy, by contrast, favors  slow development, long life span, and a prolonged period of fertility, with  greater parental investment in a few progeny.</p>
								<p><br>
									See <a href="kglossary.htm#kselection" target="_blank">K-selection</a></p></td>

							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="rachis"></a><a href="../hermaphrodite/germ line/Images/germfig1leg.htm" target="_blank">Rachis</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style4"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A large <a href="../hermaphrodite/germ line/Images/germfig2leg.htm" target="_blank">syncytial  compartment of germline cytoplasm</a> which is produced by the fusion of all  mitotic and meiotic germ cells at their basal pole  to join a common  lengthwise tube running centrally through the distal arm of the gonad. The rachis contains no nuclei, but many  mitochondria and ribosomes. Each germ  cell retains its own nucleus and  plasma membranes, so as to remain separate from surrounding germ cells. A thin version of the rachis may extend into  the loop, or possibly into the proximal gonad arm. It has also been called the &ldquo;core&rdquo; of the  gonad (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00000404;class=Paper" target="_blank">Wolf et al., 1978</a>). The rachis  occurs in both the <a href="../hermaphrodite/germ line/Images/germfig1leg.htm" target="_blank">hermaphrodite</a> and <a href="../male/germline/images/MaleReproFIG17A.jpg" target="_blank">male gonad</a> arms, and links germ cells  which can go on to become either sperm or oocytes.</p></td>

							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"></font></td>

Contraction of  radial muscles is expected to cause the pharyngeal tissue to thin, dilating the  lumen and probably enlarging the outer diameter of the pharynx. Thus the lumen  probably closes during muscle relaxation. Most pharyngeal muscles form a two cell syncytium, in which each parent  cell contains just one radial sarcomere; therefore these can be included in the  single sarcomere class. One pharyngeal  muscle, <a href="../hermaphrodite/pharynx/Images/phafig6leg.htm" target="_blank">m8</a>, appears to have smooth muscle properties rather than radial  properties.</p>

											<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/cell/cell.cgi?name=SM2L.pap;class=Cell" target="_blank">SM2R.apa</a> <br>
											<br>
								</p></td>
							<td valign=top height="111"><p>Male specific muscles  that come from two groups: the <a href="../male/musclemale/jump.html?newLink=mainframe.htm&newAnchor=Spiculeretractormuscles11" target="_blank">spicule retractors</a> (<a href="../male/musclemale/images/MaleMusFIG23.jpg" target="_blank">dsrL/R and vsrL/R</a>) and the <a href="../male/musclemale/jump.html?newLink=mainframe.htm&newAnchor=Gubernacularretractormuscles" target="_blank">gubernacular retractors</a> (<a href="../male/musclemale/images/MaleMusFIG34.jpg" target="_blank">grtL/R</a>). The <a href="../male/musclemale/images/MaleMusFIG23.jpg" target="_blank">spicule retractor muscles attach</a> to the base of each spicule and the sublateral body wall where contraction of these muscles pulls the spicules up inside the body of the animal. The <a href="../male/musclemale/images/MaleMusFIG34.jpg" target="_blank">gubernacular retractor muscles attach</a> at their ventral edges to the gubernaculum and overlying proctodeal epithelium while their dorsal edges attach to the sublateral dorsal body wall and control the positioning of the gubernaculum during copulation. </p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="#retractorcell">Retractor cell </a><br>
										See <a href="sglossary.htm#spicule" target="_blank">Spicule </a><br>
										See <a href="gglossary.htm#gubernaculum" target="_blank">Gubernaculum</a> </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="retroveiscularganglion"></a><a href="../cellIDhead1.html" target="_blank">Retrovesicular ganglion</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>RVG</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style4"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>The retrovesicular ganglion <a href="../hermaphrodite/nervous/Images/neurofig17leg.htm" target="_blank">lies at the anterior limit of  the ventral nerve cord</a>, near the ventral ganglion and nerve ring in the  head posterior to the excretory pore. It is open and continuous with the region containing the motoneurons of the ventral cord. In the early L1 this ganglion holds 12 neuron cell bodies plus one neuroblast (<a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/postemblin_1977/toc.html" target="_blank">Sulston and Horvitz, 1977</a>; <a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/MoW_built0.92/toc.html" target="_blank">White et al., 1986</a>). In the adult animal, the ganglion holds 20 neuron cell bodies. </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="reversal"></a>Reversal</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style4"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A  characteristic behavior of normal animals after sensory input to the head  (touch, heat, aversive stimuli), in which the animal stops forward motion and  immediately begins to move backwards for one or more body lengths, after which  the animal usually resumes forward motion (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00000075;class=Paper" target="_blank">Croll,  1975</a>). The are other distinctive types  of reversals, including the omega turn, and in the male mating behavior, a deep  ventral bend beginning near its tail while moving backwards.</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="dglossary.htm#deepventralbend" target="_blank">Deep ventral bend</a><br>
										See <a href="mglossary.htm#malematingbehavior" target="_blank">Male mating behavior</a> <br>
										See <a href="oglossary.htm#omegaturn" target="_blank">Omega turn</a><br>
										See <a href="pglossary.htm#pirouette" target="_blank">Pirouette</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="rhabditid"></a><a href="http://www.nyu.edu/projects/fitch/fresearch/fsystemat/fsystemat.html" target="_blank">Rhabditidae</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><p>&nbsp;</p></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>A group of <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bookshelf/br.fcgi?book=wormbook&part=phylorhab&rendertype=figure&id=A7098" target="_blank">nematode species</a> that includes a large number of ecologically and genetically diverse species, including <em>C. elegans</em>. It is a subset of the Phasmidia (Secernentea),  categorized on the basis of their mouthparts, which have given rise to many  parasitic species, including many plant parasites, as well as many free-living  soil nematodes (<a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_phylogrhabditids/phylorhab.html" target="_blank">Kiontke and  Fitch, 2005</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_evoldevnematode/evoldevnematode.html" target="_blank">Sommer, 2005</a>). </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="rhabdion"></a>Rhabdion/ Rhabdia </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>Specialized types of <a href="../hermaphrodite/interfacial/Images/interfig1leg.htm" target="_blank">cuticle  lining individual zones within the buccal passage</a>; thus the cheilostom is lined  by the cheilorhabdion, the prostom lined by the prorhabdion, etc. (Bird and  Bird, 1991, after Goodey, 1963; Van de Velde and Coomans, 1991).</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="cglossary.htm#cheilorhabdion" target="_blank">Cheilorhabdion </a><br>
										See <a href="mglossary.htm#mesorhabdion" target="_blank">Mesorhabdion</a><br>
										See <a href="mglossary.htm#metarhabdion" target="_blank">Metarhabdion</a><br>
										See <a href="pglossary.htm#prohabdion" target="_blank">Prorhabdion</a><br>
										See <a href="tglossary.htm#telorhabdion" target="_blank">Telorhabdion</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="rhabditin"></a>Rhabditin</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><p><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"></font></p></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>Birefringent crystals which  collect in the gut 4-6 hours after the first cleavage and are an early marker for gut differentiation and are thought to be products of tryptophan catabolism (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00001797;class=Paper" target="_blank">Goldstein, 1993</a>).</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="gglossary.htm#gutgranule" target="_blank">Gut granule</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="ribosome"></a><a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=stryer.figgrp.4165" target="_blank">Ribosome</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style4"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Large <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bookshelf/br.fcgi?book=wormbook&part=mechregultranslation&rendertype=figure&id=A1414" target="_blank">macromolecular complexes</a> that function  to <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=mboc4.figgrp.1077" target="_blank">translate</a> mRNAs into protein. Free  ribosomes<a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=mboc4.figgrp.1072" target="_blank"> lie within the cytoplasm</a> in loose clusters, whereas other ribosomes  are firmly attached on rough endoplasmic reticulum.&nbsp; These two classes of ribosomes generally  produce protein for different destinations. <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=mboc4.figgrp.2206" target="_blank">Free ribosomes generally produce soluble proteins</a> meant to lie within  the cytoplasm, whereas <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=mboc4.figgrp.2205" target="_blank">RER ribosomes</a> produce proteins that are first  sequestered inside the RER, or in the RER membrane, prior to export from the  cell or packaging in a cell membrane or an organelle lumen.</td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="richmedia"></a>Rich media</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><p>&nbsp;</p></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>A culture medium that  contains nutrients in excess of the minimum necessary to sustain life, normal  development and reproduction. Such additional additives might be included to promote faster growth, longer life,  or healthier activity.</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="dglossary.htm#definedmedium" target="_blank">Defined medium</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="rightroller"></a>Right roller </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Right-hand roller (S) </p></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p><a href="http://www.wormclassroom.org/movies/RolHalf.mov" target="_blank">Rolling movement</a> is caused by disruption of the normal function of collagen genes that form the cuticle such that the animals roll around a right handed helix pattern. </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><p><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="ringanddot"></a><a href="../male/rays/images/MaleRayFIG6.jpg" target="_blank">Ring and dot</a></font></strong></p></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>A staining pattern observed  by light microscopy at the tips of the rays in the developing male tail,  apparently caused by the robust adherens junctions which secure the distal  structural process to the hyp and/or  cuticle (the ring) and the distal end of the <a href="../male/rays/images/MaleRayFIG2.jpg" target="_blank">RnB</a> cilium <a href="../male/rays/images/MaleRayFIG5CD.jpg" target="_blank">to the cuticle opening</a> (the dot). Ray 6 does not show this pattern since it is the only one not open at its tip. The feature was originally noted by DIC optics, but is also  congruent with the pattern of MH27 [JAM-1] staining, and with serial thin  section data.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="ringcell"></a>Ring cell</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"></font></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p><a href="../hermaphrodite/egglaying apparatus/Images/eggfig7leg.htm" target="_blank">Vulval epithelial cells</a> (A, B1, B2, C, D, E, F) undergo <a href="../hermaphrodite/egglaying apparatus/Images/eggfig9leg.htm" target="_blank">morphological changes</a> generating a seven stacked ring formation. Specific cells migrate towards the center forming half toroid. These half toroids fuse together in a stereotyped sequence for form the vulval rings (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00004123;class=Paper" target="_blank">Shemer et al., 2000</a>).</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="vglossary.htm#vulva" target="_blank">Vulva</a> </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="rncells"></a><a href="../male/rays/images/MaleRayFIG2.jpg" target="_blank">Rn cells</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p><a href="../male/rays/images/MaleRayFIG1.jpg" target="_blank">Nine bilateral pairs of rays</a> (numbered n, 1-9). <a href="../male/rays/images/MaleRayFIG5AB.jpg" target="_blank">Each ray</a> consists of the endings of two sensory neurons (RnA and RnB) surrounded by a ray structural cell (Rnst).</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="#raycellgroup">Ray cell group</a> </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="rnai" id="rnai"></a>RNAi</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>RNA-mediated interference </td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>This refers both to a common  technique with which one can produce &ldquo;mutant&rdquo; phenotypes in wild type animals (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00003005;class=Paper" target="_blank">Fire et al., 1998</a>),  and to the normal developmental process by which an animal cell can protect  itself against invading viruses (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00026973;class=Paper" target="_blank">Schott et al., 2005</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00026709;class=Paper" target="_blank">Wilkins et al., 2005</a>) and transposable elements  (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00006203;class=Paper" target="_blank">Sijen and Plasterk, 2003</a>). In worms, individual cells can use this  process to detect invading (foreign) dsRNAs and to destroy them. As a laboratory method, the RNAi response is  utilized to trick cells into blocking the normal expression and function of the  gene of choice.</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="iglossary.htm#immuneresponse" target="_blank">Immune response</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="roamingbehavior"></a>Roaming behavior</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>Dwelling (A) </td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style4"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A behavioral state in which the  nematode tends to move forward actively at relatively high speed, with a  reduced rate of turning (<a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_chemosensation/chemosensation.html#sec3_1" target="_blank">Bargmann, 2006</a>). This behavior is equivalent to the &ldquo;rover&rdquo; behavior in<em> Drosophila </em>(<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00026600;class=Paper" target="_blank">Cheung et al., 2005</a>). Some wild strains, including the Bristol lab  strain, use this strategy when they find food to disperse themselves from their  neighbors to forage alone (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00003187;class=Paper" target="_blank">de Bono and Bargmann, 1998</a>).</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="rodlikelethality"></a>Rod-like lethality</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><p>&nbsp;</p></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>A common phenotype in  certain types of mutants (for instance in the Ras pathway) in which the animals  adopt a rigid shrunken appearance at the late L1 stage before dying. This is visibly  different from &ldquo;scrawny&rdquo; L1s in some other lethal phenotypes in which the dying  animal is also quite thin but much more  mobile.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="roller"></a><a href="http://130.15.90.245/movies/C.%20elegans%20Roller%20Mutant.mov" target="_blank">Roller</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>Rol</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>A genetic mutation affecting the cuticle structure that  causes a helical twist in the shape of the body, forcing the animal to move in  a characteristic rolling or circular motion, dominated by the shape and  handedness of the helical twist (it can be either left- or right-handed in nature). As the  animal slowly progresses forward, it tends to rotate around its long axis, and  over time the animal may be confined to a small circle on the bacterial lawn.</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="lglossary.htm#leftroller" target="_blank">Left roller</a><br>
										See<a href="#rightroller"> Right roller</a><br>
										See <a href="fglossary.htm#fiberlayers" target="_blank">Fiber layers</a><br>
										See <a href="lglossary.htm#locomotion" target="_blank">Locomotion</a><br>
										See <a href="sglossary.htm#squatphenotype" target="_blank">Squat phenotype</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="rootlet"></a>Rootlet</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"></font></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>See <a href="sglossary.htm#striatedrootlet" target="_blank">Striated rootlet </a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="rosette"></a>Rosette<a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/handbook/fig.s/GonFIG12A.jpg" target="_blank"></a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style4"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A&nbsp; briefly observed structure which appears by  DIC optics. About 10 minutes before cell division, just prior to dissolution of the nuclear membrane and the formation of the metaphase plate, the nucleoplasm surrounding the nucleolus becomes coarsely granular and can produce a rosette pattern around the nucleolus (<a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/postemblin_1977/toc.html" target="_blank">Sulston and Horvitz, 1977</a>). </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="rotation"></a>Rotation</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style4"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p><strong>1)</strong> Can refer to a  <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bookshelf/br.fcgi?book=wormbook&part=celldivision&rendertype=figure&id=A2131" target="_blank">specific step during prophase of the one cell embryo</a>, when the centrosomes and  pronuclei rotate 90&deg; in synchrony to a new orientation at the exact  center of the embryo (<a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_celldivision/celldivision.html" target="_blank">Oegema and Hyman, 2006</a>). </p>
									<p><strong>2)</strong> Can also refer to the<a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bookshelf/br.fcgi?book=wormbook&part=asymcelldiv&rendertype=figure&id=A4241" target="_blank"> early two cell stage</a>, after the centrosomes migrate onto a transverse plane, the P1 nucleus rotates 90&deg;to align the centrosomes on the AP axis (<a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_asymcelldiv/asymcelldiv.html" target="_blank">G&ouml;nczy and Rose, 2005</a>).</p>
							  <p><br>
									See <a href="cglossary.htm#centration" target="_blank">Centration</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="roughendoplasmicreticulum"></a><a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=mboc4.figgrp.2205" target="_blank">Rough endoplasmic reticulum</a><a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/query.fcgi?cmd=Search&db=books&doptcmdl=GenBookHL&term=rough+ER+AND+mboc4%5Bbook%5D+AND+373481%5Buid%5D&rid=mboc4.figgrp.2205" target="_blank"> </a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Rough ER<br>
								RER</p></td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style4"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A subset of the endoplasmic  reticulum that is studded with ribosomes (giving it the rough appearance) and  is generally in continuity with the nuclear envelope. In neurons, most RER lie in the cell body, but some may  also extend into cell processes and even this distant domain remains in  continuity with that in the cell body (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00005269;class=Paper" target="_blank">Rolls et al., 2002</a>).</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="rubberbandphenotype"></a>Rubberband phenotype </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style4"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A characteristic  body-shortening behavior seen in some mutations affecting neuromuscular  signaling where the entire body undergoes coordinate contraction of all bodywall  muscles in synchrony to shorten the animal, and then coordinate relaxation to  restore the animal to full length (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00001525;class=Paper" target="_blank">Levin and Horvitz, 1992</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00006135;class=Paper" target="_blank">de la Cruz et al., 2003</a>).</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="cglossary.htm#convulsion" target="_blank">Convulsion</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="run" id="run"></a>Run</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style4"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A period of forward motion that is not interrupted by  a reversal. Different behavioral states  tend to produce either short runs, or mostly long runs (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00003740;class=Paper" target="_blank">Pierce-Shimomura et  al., 1999</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_behavior/behavior.html#sec7_6" target="_blank">Lockery</a> in <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_behavior/behavior.html" target="_blank">Hart, 2006</a>)</p></td>
						</tr>
