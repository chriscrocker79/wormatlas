							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="t"></a><a href="../male/hypodermis/images/MaleHypFIG1.jpg" target="_blank">T cell</a> </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><p><a 
href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/cell/cell.cgi?name=FLPL;class=Cell"></a>Tail precursor cell (S) </p></td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"><a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/cell/cell.cgi?name=tl;class=Cell" target="_blank">TL</a><br>
									<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/cell/cell.cgi?name=tr;class=Cell" target="_blank">TR</a> </span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Tail lateral ectoblasts  that give rise to neurons, hypodermis and glial lineages. While initially identical, after the L2 lethargus <a href="../male/hypodermis/images/MaleHypFIG3A.jpg" target="_blank">the T cell lineages</a> become markedly different  between <a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/postemblin_1977/art/fl19.jpg" target="_blank">males</a> and <a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/postemblin_1977/art/fl12.jpg" target="_blank">hermaphrodites</a> helping to provide the sexually dimorphic tail structures found in males. </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="ttubule"></a><a href="../hermaphrodite/muscleintro/Images/musfig2leg.htm" target="_blank">T tubule </a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>Transverse tubule (S) </td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A  tubular  compartment found in vertebrate muscle cells, extending inward from the plasma membrane in the  region near the sarcomeres, probably separate from the SER compartment. These tubules are thought to form a network of membranes that penetrate and span the cross section of each muscle promoting the propagation of  contractile (electrical) signals from the surface membrane directly to the  region of the muscle filaments. Identical structures have not been identified in <em>C. elegans</em>, but there is a component of sarcoplasmic reticulum in <a href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/MusSomaticframeset.html" target="_blank">bodywall muscle</a> which may serve parallel functions, running as thin tubules near the <a href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig10leg.htm" target="_blank">sarcomeres</a> and the <a href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig11leg.htm" target="_blank">dense bodies</a>. </p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="sglossary.htm#sarcoplasmicreticulum" target="_blank">Sarcoplasmic reticulum</a> </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tadpolestage"></a><a href="../hermaphrodite/introduction/IMAGES/introfig7leg.htm" target="_blank">Tadpole stage</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>1.5 fold stage (S) </td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A &quot;tadpole&quot; looking embryonic stage in which the late gastrula consists of an  enlarged anterior end (stomodeum) with a narrower, and more opaque, bit of tail  lying just behind it within the eggshell.&nbsp;The elongation phase (and continued cellular morphogenesis)  gradually converts the tadpole-like gastrula into a larval shape and the buccal cavity becomes apparent.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="tail"></a><a href="../hermaphrodite/introduction/IMAGES/introfig3leg.htm" target="_blank">Tail</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>The most posterior region of  the body. The anterior bound of the tail  is ill defined though it might be marked as far forward as the position of the  post-deirids, or as far posterior as the opening of the anus.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tailbleb"></a>Tail bleb <a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/handbook/fig.s/GerFIG3D.jpg" target="_blank"></a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"></font></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>Any irregular swelling of  the extreme tail tip, usually caused by a local defect in the normal smooth  tapering of the hypodermis and/or cuticle.</p></td>
						</tr>

sans-serif"><a name="tailcurling"></a>Tail curling <a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/Ware_build0.2/art/fig17_large.jpg" target="_blank"></a></font></strong></td>

							<td valign=top><b><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tailknob"></a>Tail knob </font></strong></font></b></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A common mutant phenotype  in which the thin tapering of the tail whip is interrupted by a bulbous growth,  often near the extreme tip (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00027716;class=Paper" target="_blank">Rio-Albrectsen et al., 2006</a>). </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><b><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tailseam"></a>Tail seam </font></strong></font></b></td>
							<td valign=top><p>&nbsp;</p></td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>The more posterior members  of the seam cells along the lateral bodywall which do not fuse with the hypodermal syncytium.&nbsp;In the<a href="../male/hypodermis/MaleHypframeset.html" target="_blank"> adult male seam</a>, the 3 most posterior members on each side (<a href="../male/hypodermis/images/MaleHypFIG1.jpg" target="_blank">V5, V6 and T</a>) follow a  different<a href="../male/hypodermis/images/MaleHypFIG3A.jpg" target="_blank"> cell fate</a> from the other seam cells, acting as stem cells to create  the sensory rays. In late L4 the posterior daughters of the ray precursor cells (<a href="../male/hypodermis/images/MaleHypFIG3A.jpg" target="_blank">R1.p-R5.p</a>) <a href="../male/hypodermis/images/MaleHypFIG5.jpg" target="_blank">fuse together, appear seam-like and are called the tail seam SET</a> (<a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/Sulston%20postembmale_1980/toc.html" target="_blank">Sulston et al., 1980</a>). The tail seam does not produce alae.</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="sglossary.htm#set" target="_blank">SET</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tailspike"></a><a href="../hermaphrodite/atypical/jump.html?newLink=mainframe.htm&newAnchor=Tailspikescaffoldcells2" target="_blank">Tail spike </a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26">ABplppppppa<br>
								ABprppppppa</span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A <a href="../hermaphrodite/atypical/Images/atypfig4leg.htm" target="_blank">pair of short-lived hyp  cells</a> in the tail tip which fuse to form a syncytium and then form a slender bundle of  filaments over which the tail tip cuticle forms. These cells undergo cell death  after 6 hours, leaving behind a narrow spike of cuticle (<a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/Sulstonemblin_1983/toc.html" target="_blank">Sulston et al.,  1983</a>). This thin cuticle specialization  is often called the tail spike, while the cells may be called the tail spike  cells. The posterior process of the tail  spike  has the appearance of <a href="../hermaphrodite/atypical/Images/atypfig4leg.htm" target="_blank">a cilium filled with microtubules and  wrapped by hyp10</a>.</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="#tailtip">Tail tip</a><br>
										See <a href="sglossary.htm#scaffoldcell" target="_blank">Scaffold cell</a></p></td>

						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tailtip"></a><a href="../hermaphrodite/introduction/IMAGES/introfig3leg.htm" target="_blank">Tail tip</a> </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>Tail spike (S) </td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td align="left" valign=top><p>The most posterior portion  of the tail, usually referring to that portion lying posterior to the phasmid  openings. In all <a href="http://www.nyu.edu/projects/fitch/Wildtyp.gif" target="_blank">hermaphrodites and in younger males</a> the tail tip adopts a very  long tapered shape ending in a thin spike that may represent some 10-15% of the  total body length (proportionately longer in hermaphrodites than in young  males). The tail whip is the finest,  thinnest part of the tail tip, more flexible than that portion near the  phasmids.</p>
									<p>The tail tip is initially shaped by the <a href="../hermaphrodite/atypical/jump.html?newLink=mainframe.htm&newAnchor=Tailspikescaffoldcells2" target="_blank">tail spike cells</a> which undergo programmed cell death late in embryogenesis and leave a narrow spike of cuticle. </p>
								<p>During the maturation of the male, <a href="../male/hypodermis/images/MaleHypFIG5.jpg" target="_blank">the tail tip undergoes  retraction</a> in late L4 stage and then <a href="../male/hypodermis/images/MaleHypFIG10.jpg" target="_blank">hypodermal remodeling</a> to form the fan, rays, bursa, and copulatory  apparatus of the <a href="../male/epithelial/images/MaleEpiFIG1.jpg" target="_blank">adult male tail</a>.&nbsp;Sensory endings of the immature male tail (such as those of <a href="../neurons/Individual Neurons/PHCframeset.html" target="_blank">PHC</a> and<a href="../neurons/Individual Neurons/PLMframeset.html" target="_blank"> PLM</a>)  also become retracted and no longer extend into the posterior fan in the adult.</p>
								<p><br>
									See <a href="rglossary.htm#retraction" target="_blank">Retraction</a><br>
									See <a href="#tailwhip">Tail whip</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tailwaving"></a>Tail waving </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica,

						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><b><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="tailwhip"></a>Tail whip </font></b></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"></font></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>The thinnest part of the  tail tip of the hermaphrodite, at the extreme posterior end, which is so narrow that it easily bends  and flexes upon itself as the animal moves. The tail whip contains a few putative sensory endings (<a href="../neurons/Individual Neurons/PHCframeset.html" target="_blank">PHC</a>, nonciliated)  surrounded only by a thin coating of hypodermis and the cuticle. These neuronal endings probably bend by 180&deg; when the animal reverses direction, and may be activated by this bending.</p>
									<p><br>
										See<a href="#tailtip"> Tail tip</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tapresponse"></a><a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_behavior/behavior.html#sec2_6" target="_blank">Tap response </a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif">Tap reflex (S) </font></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>A form of touch  response that is elicited by repetitively hitting the culture plate to  stimulate a behavioral response involving the touch dendrites. One can see this response by tapping the  plate by hand, or hitting it against the microscope stage, but mechanical  devices have been built to generate very reproducible repetitive taps (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00001251;class=Paper" target="_blank">Rankin  et al., 1990</a>). It is an excellent means  to study behavioral <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_behavior/behavior.html#sec5_1" target="_blank">habituation </a>and dishabituation (<a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_behavior/behavior.html#sec2_6" target="_blank">Rankin, 2005</a>). </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="taste"></a>Taste<a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/query.fcgi?cmd=Search&db=books&doptcmdl=GenBookHL&term=paramyosin+AND+ce2%5Bbook%5D+AND+1479%5Buid%5D&rid=ce2.figgrp.547" target="_blank"></a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A form of chemosensation by which <em>C. elegans</em> can detect food and other environmental cues (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00006481;class=Paper" target="_blank">Hilliard et al., 2004</a>). Taste and olfaction have been used to learning and habituation assays (<a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_behavior/behavior.html#sec4" target="_blank">Jansen, 2005</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_behavior/behavior.html#sec5_7" target="_blank">Law and van der Kooy, 2005</a>) . </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="taxis"></a>Taxis</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"></font></td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>An action that results in the  animal orienting in response to a stimulus. This can be to move towards it (positive taxis)  or away from it (negative taxis). Positive chemotaxis usually results in the  animal traveling towards a higher concentration of a chemical signal. <em>C. elegans</em> has been shown to exhibit  chemotaxis, phototaxis and thermotaxis.&nbsp;The regular motions of <em>C. elegans</em> on a culture plate may be a rather  complex mixture of taxes and kineses (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00000455;class=Paper" target="_blank">Dusenbery, 1980</a>).</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="aglossary.htm#approachbehavior" target="_blank">Approach behavior</a><br>
										See <a href="aglossary.htm#avoidancebehavior" target="_blank">Avoidance behavior</a><br>
										See <a href="cglossary.htm#chemotaxis" target="_blank">Chemotaxis</a><br>
										See <a href="gglossary.htm#galvanotaxis" target="_blank">Galvanotaxis</a><br>
										See <a href="kglossary.htm#kinesis" target="_blank">Kinesis</a><br>
										See <a href="pglossary.htm#phototaxis" target="_blank">Phototaxis</a><br>
										See <a href="sglossary.htm#switchedresponse" target="_blank">Switched response</a><br>
										See <a href="#thermotaxis">Thermotaxis</a><br>
										See <a href="#tropotaxis">Tropotaxis</a></p></td>
						</tr>

							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="telamon"></a>Telamon</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>Archaic term for a lateral portion of the  cloacal cuticle enclosing the spicule channels.  It is sclerotized to reinforce the channel, and together with the  gubernaculum, it helps to direct the spicule extension outward. Not clear how the telamon is separated from  the gubernaculum, except that the gubernaculum proper lies more dorsal and  medial. </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="telomere"></a><a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=cooper.figgrp.635" target="_blank">Telomere</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><p>&nbsp;</p></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>The distal end of a  linear chromosome arm. The two endpoints of  each chromosome are characterized by distinctive repetitive DNA sequences  (TTAGGC) of 4-9 kb which may act to cap each chromosome and protect them from fusion and degradation (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00002535;class=Paper" target="_blank">Wicky et al., 1996</a>). <em><a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/gene/gene?name=WBGene00006618;class=Gene" target="_blank">trt-1</a></em> has been identified as the <em>C. elegans</em> telomerase reverse transcriptase and is required to maintain telomere ends through successive generations (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00027101;class=Paper" target="_blank">Meier et al., 2006</a>). While it appears that organismal adult life span is independent of telomere length (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/gene/gene?name=WBGene00006618;class=Gene" target="_blank">Raices et al., 2005</a>), maintenance of telomere length is required to preserve  germ line immortality through successive generations (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00003886;class=Paper" target="_blank">Ahmed and Hodgkin, 2000</a>).</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="telophase"></a><a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bookshelf/br.fcgi?book=wormbook&part=celldivision&rendertype=figure&id=A2131" target="_blank">Telophase</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><p>&nbsp;</p></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>A subdivision of<a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=mboc4.figgrp.3346" target="_blank"> M phase</a>,  during mitosis, during which separated daughter chromosomes concentrate at the  two mitotic spindle poles and become surrounded by the reformed nuclear  envelope.</td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><b><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="telorhabdion" id="telorhabdion"></a>Telorhabdion</font></b></td>

This portion of the cilium has  sometimes been loosely referred to as a basal body (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00000822;class=Paper" target="_blank">Wright, 1980</a>).
				        <p><strong>2)</strong> Alternately, a <a href="../hermaphrodite/germ line/Images/germfig3A_Dleg.htm" target="_blank">segment of the distal arm of the germline</a> where germ cells progress from continued mitosis (in the more distal portion)  and enter into the <a href="../hermaphrodite/germ line/Images/germfig3A_Dleg.htm" target="_blank">pachytene phase</a> (in the more proximal portion<a href="../hermaphrodite/somatic gonad/Images/somaticfig5leg.htm" target="_blank"> under sheath  cell 1</a>) (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00002045;class=Paper" target="_blank">Crittenden et al., 1994</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00003990;class=Paper" target="_blank">Hubbard and Greenstein,  2000</a>). It remains unclear whether the  filapodial extensions of sheath cell pair 1 (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00003617;class=Paper" target="_blank">Hall et al., 1999</a>) touch the  transition zone, or only the pachytene zone. </p>
								<p><br>
									See <a href="bglossary.htm#basalbody" target="_blank">Basal body</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="transporterprotein"></a>Transporter protein </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A membrane-associated  macromolecule that serves to move certain categories of solutes across the  membrane, from inside to the outside, or vice versa. Some transporters may constitute a patent  channel in the membrane while others may enfold the solute molecules and carry them  across the membrane.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="transportvesicle"></a>Transport vesicle <a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/handbook/fig.s/CutFIG8B.jpg" target="_blank"></a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><p>&nbsp;</p></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>A category of membrane-bound small vesicle that is  specialized for moving components to the cell periphery (especially down the  axon) in a cell, but which may require further maturation to become functional  (for instance, before being competent as a <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bookshelf/br.fcgi?book=wormbook&part=synapticfunction&rendertype=figure&id=A6279" target="_blank">synaptic vesicle</a>).</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="vglossary.htm#vesicle" target="_blank">Vesicle</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="transverse"></a>Transverse</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>Transverse aspect (S) </td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>A plane of section (or  angle of view) perpendicular to the longitudinal body axis. It is also called &quot;cross section&quot;. </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="treadmilling" id="treadmilling"></a><a href="http://www.mcb.arizona.edu/wardlab/treadingvid.html" target="_blank">Treadmilling</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>An inferred mechanism for  amoeboid motion, in which the components at the leading edge of the pseudopod  become attached to the substrate and then are translocated backwards along the  bottom edge of the pseudopod, pulling the cell (or growth cone) forward, and  then being released from the substrate to be internalized and recycled back to  the leading edge (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00000558;class=Paper" target="_blank">Roberts and Ward, 1982</a>).</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="pglossary.htm#pseudopodelongation" target="_blank">Pseudopod elongation </a><br>
										See <a href="sglossary.htm#spermmotility" target="_blank">Sperm motility </a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="triadicsynapse"></a><a href="../images/polyadicsynapses.jpg" target="_blank">Triadic synapse</a> </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><p>&nbsp;</p>
									<p>&nbsp;</p></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>A<a href="../hermaphrodite/nervous/Images/neurofig21leg.htm" target="_blank"> chemical synapse</a> in which  the presynaptic profile appears to have access to three postsynaptic profiles  simultaneously; thus A --&gt; B+C+D.</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="dglossary.htm#dyadicsynapse" target="_blank">Dyadic synapse</a><br>
										See <a href="mglossary.htm#monadicsynapse" target="_blank">Monadic synapse</a> </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tropomyosin"></a><a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26888/figure/A3013/" target="_blank">Tropomyosin</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A large rod-shaped protein  closely associated with the actin thin filament in muscle cells (Miller et al.,  1983). </p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="#thinfilament">Thin filament </a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="troponin"></a><a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26888/figure/A3013/" target="_blank">Troponin</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top><p>&nbsp;</p>
									<p>&nbsp;</p></td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A protein complex  associated with tropomyosin within the actin thin filament. It consists of  three gene products, troponin T, troponin I and troponin C (TnT, TnI,  TnC). Troponins<a href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig12leg.htm" target="_blank"> are expressed with  bodywall muscles</a> and pharyngeal muscles in <em>C. elegans</em>, and may play a key role  in actin/myosin interactions within the muscle sarcomere. </p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="#thinfilament">Thin filament</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tropotaxis"></a>Tropotaxis<a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/query.fcgi?cmd=Search&db=books&doptcmdl=GenBookHL&term=plus+end+AND+mboc4%5Bbook%5D+AND+373953%5Buid%5D&rid=mboc4.figgrp.2973" target="_blank"></a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Any locomotory  behavior in which the animal orients its direction by comparing the signal  strength at two paired sense organs simultaneously. Since nematode sensilla pairs tend to be  left/right pairs, and the animal lies on its side (bringing both sensilla into  virtually the same point in any gradient within the plane of body motion),  tropotaxis seems an unlikely means to recognize any signal gradient, unless the  animal compares signals between head and tail receptors (amphid vs phasmid).</p>
									<p><br>
										See <a href="kglossary.htm#klinotaxis" target="_blank">Klinotaxis</a><br>
										See <a href="#taxis">Taxis</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tubularbody"></a>Tubular body <a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/postemblin_1977/art/fl18.jpg" target="_self"></a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Dendritic specialization  within the <a href="../neurons/Individual Neurons/CEPframeset.html" target="_blank">CEP</a> mechanosensillum.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tubularradii"></a><a href="../hermaphrodite/pharynx/Images/phafig3E_Gleg.htm" target="_blank">Tubular radii </a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>Pharyngeal channels (S) </td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>See <a href="pglossary.htm#pharyngealchannels" target="_blank">Pharyngeal channels </a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tubule"></a>Tubule<a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_celldivision/celldivisionfig2.jpg" target="_blank"></a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>Dark thin structures,&nbsp;&ldquo;tubules&rdquo;, form tight networks in  several categories of pharyngeal muscle cells at regions of apparent  neuromuscular synaptic contact. One  pharyngeal neuron soma (M2) contains similar material. It is not clear whether these objects are  solid or membranous (<a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/albertson_1975/toc.html" target="_blank">Albertson and Thomson, 1976</a>). Dark vesicular structures are also found near  these networks. These tubules were also  called &ldquo;striations&rdquo; or &ldquo;reticulations&rdquo;  by the same authors.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tubuleassociatedmaterial"></a><a href="../hermaphrodite/neuronalsupport/Images/neurofig30leg.htm" target="_blank">Tubule-associated material </a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>TAM</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p><a href="../hermaphrodite/neuronalsupport/Images/neurofig31leg.htm" target="_blank">Electron dense material</a> which lies in the cytoplasm of sensory cilia in close apposition to a  microtubule bundle in several sensory neurons, including <a href="../neurons/Individual Neurons/CEPframeset.html" target="_blank">CEP</a>, <a href="../neurons/Individual Neurons/OLLframeset.html" target="_blank">OLL</a> and <a href="../neurons/Individual Neurons/OLQframeset.html" target="_blank">OLQ</a>. </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tubulin"></a><a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=mboc4.figgrp.2968" target="_blank">Tubulin</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>&nbsp;</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="tumorousgermline"></a>Tumorous germline </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A mutant phenotype in which  germline proliferation continues unabated (through endless mitotic divisions)  to produce an excess of immature germ cells, enlarging the gonad into a swollen  tumorous organ which can distort the whole body of the nematode, or spill  across the basal lamina to invade neighboring tissues (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00002730;class=Paper" target="_blank">Wilson Berry et al., 1997</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00003093;class=Paper" target="_blank">Kadyk and Kimble, 1998</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00006014;class=Paper" target="_blank">Huang et al., 2003</a>).</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="turgorpressure"></a>Turgor pressure </font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>Hydrostatic pressure (S) </td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>The internal pressure of body fluids that is transmitted outward  to the body cuticle in all directions to fully inflate the animal. In the nematode, the action of body muscle  contractions utilizes turgor pressure to help spread their forces over  the whole body length to produce a general undulatory motion. The <a href="../hermaphrodite/excretory/Excframeset.html" target="_blank">excretory system</a> may act to regulate salt  balances in a manner that modifies turgor pressure which, when defective, can lead  to animals with flaccid paralysis or rigid rod-like paralysis. High turgor pressure may generally lead to  narrowing of all tubular organs, so that egg-laying and defecation require the  action of dilator muscles to open the related passageways, and the use of  spicules in male mating to open the hermaphrodite vulva (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00000255;class=Paper" target="_blank">Nicholas, 1975</a>).</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="turning"></a>Turning</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p><em>C. elegans</em> forward locomotion involves episodes of turning that are termed &quot;pirouettes&quot;. These are &quot;sharp&quot; turns with the intervals of forward movement between them termed &quot;runs&quot; (<a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_behavior/behavior.html#sec7" target="_blank">Ringstad and Horvitz</a> in <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_behavior/behavior.html" target="_blank">Hart, 2006</a>). </p>
									<p>Turning is also a distinctive event during  male mating behavior when the male reaches the edge of  the hermaphrodite&rsquo;s body and must continue backing along the other side of the  hermaphrodite, in the opposite net direction in search of the vulva (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00001861;class=Paper" target="_blank">Loer and  Kenyon, 1993</a>). Each turn can be scored  as &ldquo;good&rdquo;, &ldquo;sloppy&rdquo; or &ldquo;missed&rdquo; according to how well the male remains in  contact with the body of the hermaphrodite during the turning event (<a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_behavior/behavior.html#sec9_7" target="_blank">Loer</a> in <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_behavior/behavior.html#sec9_7" target="_blank">Hart , 2006</a>). Some missed turns can be further subdivided  into &ldquo;swim off&rdquo;, &ldquo;wide turns&rdquo;, or &ldquo;stutter turns&rdquo; (<a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_behavior/behavior.html#sec10_4" target="_blank">Emmons</a> in <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_behavior/behavior.html" target="_blank">Hart, 2006</a>).</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="twitcherphenotype" id="twitcherphenotype"></a>Twitcher phenotype <a href="http://www.wormatlas.org/ver1/handbook/excretory.htm#ductandporecells" target="_parent"></a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A<a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK19756/figure/A1058/?report=objectonly" target="_blank"> defect in some unc mutants</a> (ie. <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/gene/gene?name=WBGene00006759;class=Gene" target="_blank"><em>unc-22</em></a>) in which individual muscles or local portions of one muscle quadrant  show rapid, independent contractions, superimposed on the well coordinated  smooth motor contractions pass along the length of the body, that characterize  normal body motion.</p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">

sans-serif"><a name="twitchin"></a>Twitchin</font></strong></td>
