						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="saltatory"></a>Saltatory</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A characteristic jerky motion involving  abrupt transitions between motion and immobility. Motions of a certain speed continue for short  duration, alternating with periods of immobility. Such dynamics are typical of some objects under the control  of molecular motors, whose activity may rapidly switch between ON and OFF  states as is during axonal transport of dense core vesicles (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00013484;class=Paper" target="_blank">Zhan et al., 2004</a>).</p>
									<p>Also can be used to describe the evolutionary theory that reveals discontinuous, large jumps in the evolution of a species (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00000731;class=Paper" target="_blank">Sternberg and Horvitz, 1984</a>). </p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica,

							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"></font></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>Plasma  membrane of a muscle cell, covering the outisde of myofilament network, the muscle belly and the muscle arms. </p></td>

						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><p><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"><a name="sarcomere"></a><a href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig10leg.htm" target="_blank">Sarcomere</a></font></strong></p></td>
							<td valign=top><font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif">Spindle (archaic) </font></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p>A single <a href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig9leg.htm" target="_blank">functional unit</a> in  the contractile portion of a muscle cell. <a href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/MusSomaticframeset.html" target="_blank">Body muscle</a> is obliquely striated  with multiple sarcomeres in repeating fashion. <a href="../hermaphrodite/musclenonstriated/jump.html?newLink=mainframe.htm&newAnchor=Pharyngealmuscles2" target="_blank">Pharyngeal muscle</a> is radial but  not striated, with only a few sarcomeres per cell. There are many specialized muscles in <a href="../male/muscleintro/images/MaleMusFIG1.jpg" target="_blank">male tail</a> and in the <a href="../hermaphrodite/egglaying apparatus/Images/eggfig12leg.htm" target="_blank">vulva</a> which are diagonal but not striated, and often consist of  only a single sarcomere. There are several muscle sarcomeres that are organized  circumferentially, in particular,  the sphincter and one of the <a href="../hermaphrodite/egglaying apparatus/Images/eggfig11Aleg.htm" target="_blank">uterine</a> muscles.</p>
									<p>Striations consist of oriented members of  the <a href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig9leg.htm" target="_blank">myofilament lattice</a>, which are anchored to <a href="../hermaphrodite/musclesomatic/Images/musfig13leg.htm" target="_blank">dense bodies on the plasma  membrane</a>. The orientation of the lattice  elements (oblique to the body axis, or diagonal, or radial) determine the angle  of the striations and of the whole muscle. Some  <a href="../hermaphrodite/musclenonstriated/MusNonstriframeset.html" target="_blank">smooth muscles</a> in the nematode appear to be organized without recognizable  sarcomeres, such as the <a href="../hermaphrodite/musclenonstriated/jump.html?newLink=mainframe.htm&newAnchor=Contractilegonadsheath8" target="_blank">gonadal sheath</a> and the <a href="../hermaphrodite/musclenonstriated/jump.html?newLink=mainframe.htm&newAnchor=Analsphinctermuscle5" target="_blank">sphincter muscles</a>.<br>
										&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; </p>
								<p>See <a href="dglossary.htm#densebody" target="_blank">Dense body</a><br>
									See <a href="mglossary.htm#myofilamentlattice" target="_blank">Myofilament lattice</a><br>
									See <a href="#smoothmuscle">Smooth muscle</a><br>
									See <a href="zglossary.htm#zband" target="_blank">Z band</a></p></td>

These structures are quite thin and appear  discontinuous in thin section due to their tortuosity in <em>C. elegans</em>.</p>

							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="scaffold"></a>Scaffold</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>This term can apply to  similar structures  at a variety of different levels, from tissues, to  cells, to cell parts, to molecules.</p>
									<p>Provided  by sheath cell processes for axonal outgrowth (<a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=ce2.section.718" target="_blank">Antebi et al., 1997</a>) and by <a href="../hermaphrodite/muscleGLR/MusGLRframeset.html" target="_blank">GLRs</a> for muscle arm outgrowth.</p>
								<p>Alternately,  the same term can refer to subcellular features. Some plasma membranes are reinforced by  &ldquo;scaffolds&rdquo; either on the outside by 1) basement membrane on the basal surface,  or by 2) glycocalyx on the apical surface, or 3) reinforced on the cytoplasmic  side by surface coats made from cortical cytoskeleton, spectrin and cytoplasmic  linkers, as well as a variety of uncharacterized molecules, many of which add visible  electron density to the inner leaflet when viewed by TEM.</p>
								<p>Cytoskeletal elements such as microtubules or  intermediate filaments or PSD-containing  subsynaptic proteins may be &ldquo;decorated&rdquo; by specific proteins over their length,  thus acting as a scaffold onto which are positioned microtubule-associated  proteins (MAPs), junctional proteins, synaptic receptors, etc.</p>
								<p><br>
									See <a href="bglossary.htm#basallamina" target="_blank">Basal lamina</a><br>
									See <a href="gglossary.htm#glycocalyx" target="_blank">Glycocalyx</a><br>
									See <a href="#spectrin">Spectrin</a><br>
									See <a href="uglossary.htm#undercoat" target="_blank">Undercoat</a></p></td>
						</tr>
						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="scaffoldcell"></a>Scaffold cell</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td align="left" valign=top><p>A cell whose cell body  and/or extended processes provide the substrate which acts to shape the  differentiation of another tissue. Several different cell types fit this  general description:</p>
									<p><strong>1)</strong> The <a href="../hermaphrodite/muscleGLR/MusGLRframeset.html" target="_blank">GLR</a> cell bodies mark the position at which head muscles project their arms  inward and the <a href="../hermaphrodite/muscleGLR/Images/glrfig3E_Ileg.htm" target="_blank">GLRs&rsquo; extended processes act as the substrate</a> over which these  muscle arms form a thin cylindrical plexus or motor plate onto which the nerve  ring motor neurons form neuromuscular junctions.</p>
								<p><strong>2)</strong> The <a href="../hermaphrodite/atypical/jump.html?newLink=mainframe.htm&newAnchor=Tailspikescaffoldcells2" target="_blank">spike cells</a> of the tail tip, and their epithelial  covering by <a href="../hermaphrodite/hypodermis/Images/hypfig12leg.htm" target="_blank">hyp10</a>, also fit this definition.  The spike cell syncytium acts as a scaffold for a posterior extension of  hyp10, while the hyp10 extension acts as a scaffold for the secreted spike of  cuticle at the extreme tail tip.</p>
								<p><strong>3)</strong> The <a href="../hermaphrodite/atypical/jump.html?newLink=mainframe.htm&newAnchor=XXX1" target="_blank">XXX cells</a> of the lateral lips and their extended anterior  processes seem to provide a scaffold for the early elongation of the ventral  bodywall muscles, and for the separate paths of the amphid nerve and the  mechanosensory nerves. They may also organize the relative positions of the  various socket cells of the lateral lips. </p>
								<p><strong>4)</strong> The <a href="../hermaphrodite/egglaying apparatus/Images/eggfig8leg.htm" target="_blank">anchor cell</a> (AC) of the vulva acts as a scaffold for the  formation of the<a href="../hermaphrodite/egglaying apparatus/Images/eggfig5ABleg.htm" target="_blank"> uterine seam (utse)</a> in order to <a href="../hermaphrodite/egglaying apparatus/Images/eggfig3leg.htm" target="_blank">connect the vulval passageway  to the two lobes of the uterus</a>.</p></td>
						</tr>

sans-serif"></font></td>

							<td valign=top background="#lamellipodium"><p>A local hardening of the cuticle, usually  accompanied by distinctively darker, more electron dense specialization,  sometimes running uniformly across the layers, or sometimes showing parallel  striations across the layers. Such  specialization is most dramatic at the <a href="../male/hook/images/MaleHookFIG2BC.jpg" target="_blank">hook</a> and <a href="../male/spicules/images/MaleSpicFIG6.jpg" target="_blank">spicules</a> of the male tail,  which are used as <a href="../movies/Spicules.mpg" target="_blank">physical anchorages</a> during male <a href="../male/neuronalsupport/images/MaleNeuroFIG2.jpg" target="_blank">mating behavior</a>. They are also  found in many other portions of cuticle that are subject to high stress, such  as along the <a href="../hermaphrodite/cuticle/Images/CutFIG7B.jpg" target="_blank">buccal </a>and <a href="../hermaphrodite/cuticle/Images/CutFIG7C.jpg" target="_blank">pharyngeal</a> passageways and the <a href="../hermaphrodite/cuticle/Images/CutFIG7E.jpg" target="_blank">grinder</a>, or at the <a href="../hermaphrodite/cuticle/Images/cutfig8leg.htm" target="_blank">excretory pore</a>.</p></td>

										See <a href="aglossary.htm#adherensjunction" target="_blank">Adherens junction</a></p></td>

						<tr class="style18">
							<td valign=top><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="seamcell"></a><a href="../hermaphrodite/seam cells/Seamframeset.html" target="_blank">Seam cell</a></font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>This term most often refers to the hypodermal seam cells, which<a href="../hermaphrodite/seam cells/Images/Seamfig2leg.htm" target="_blank"> lie along the lateral  bodywall</a> to form longitudinal stripes of epithelial cells <a href="../hermaphrodite/seam cells/Images/Seamfig3leg.htm" target="_blank">underlying the alae</a>,  and are apparently responsible for formation of the alae themselves (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00000354;class=Paper" target="_blank">Singh and  Sulston, 1978</a>). These differentiated  cells also act as blast cells that can later give rise to specialized sensory  structures such as the male tail rays.  In late larval development, the seam cells mature and fuse to form a <a href="../hermaphrodite/seam cells/Images/Seamfig1leg.htm" target="_blank">continuous syncytium</a> along the bodywall, separate from the <a href="../hermaphrodite/hypodermis/Images/hypfig5leg.htm" target="_blank">hyp 7 syncytium</a>.</p>
									<p>The  term can also refer to the <a href="../hermaphrodite/egglaying apparatus/Images/eggfig2leg.htm" target="_blank">uterine seam</a> (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/ontology/anatomy?name=utse" target="_blank">utse</a>), another epithelium that acts to  bind the vulval epithelium to the uterine epithelium, forming a three-way  junction linking the single vulval passageway to the anterior and posterior  lobes of the reproductive tract. This  structure is created by the fusion of several precursor cells, and initially  forms over the anchor cell, which acts as a scaffold.</p></td>
						</tr>

							<td valign=top><p><strong><font face="Arial, Helvetica, 
sans-serif"><a name="sexdetermination"></a>Sex determination</font></strong></p></td>
							<td valign=top><p>&nbsp;</p>
									<font face="Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif"></font></td>
							<td valign=top>&nbsp;</td>
							<td valign=top><p><em>C. elegans</em> has<a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bookshelf/br.fcgi?book=wormbook&part=somaticsexdeterm&rendertype=figure&id=A3231" target="_blank"> two sexes</a>. Hermaphrodites are diploid with five pairs of autosomes and two X chromosomes. Males have the same number of autosomes, but only have one X chromosome. Gametes produced by hermaphrodites are all haplo X so they give rise to nearly all XX hermaphrodite self progeny except for the rare male formed from X-chromosome loss. Males produce haplo-X and nullo-X sperm so cross progeny with a hermaphrodite will produce half male progeny.</p>
									<p>Most differences between the sexes develop postembryonically by different cell-specific patterns of gene expression. <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bookshelf/br.fcgi?book=wormbook&part=somaticsexdeterm&rendertype=figure&id=A3232" target="_blank">In somatic cells</a>, the sex-specific fate of different cells is controlled by the master regulator <em><a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/gene/gene?name=WBGene00006604;class=Gene" target="_blank">tra-1</a></em> which acts as a <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bookshelf/br.fcgi?book=wormbook&part=somaticsexdeterm&rendertype=figure&id=A3233" target="_blank">transcriptional repressor in hermaphrodites</a>. <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bookshelf/br.fcgi?book=wormbook&part=sexgermline&rendertype=figure&id=A16232" target="_blank">In the germ line</a>, regulatory interactions of additional genes are required to specify sexual fate. </p>
								<p>For more information see chapters in Wormbook (<a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_introsexdetermination/introsexdetermination.html" target="_blank">Herman, 2005</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_sexgermline.2/sexgermline.html" target="_blank">Ellis and Schedl, 2007</a>; <a href="http://www.wormbook.org/chapters/www_somaticsexdeterm/somaticsexdeterm.html" target="_blank">Zarkower, 2006</a>).</p></td>

sans-serif"><a name="stemcell"></a>Stem cell</font></strong></td>
							<td valign=top>Blast cell (S) </td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>In  higher animals this term usually refers to a cell that remains undifferentiated  into juvenile or adult life and which is still capable of further (unlimited) cell  divisions to later produce a variety of different cell types (perhaps being  totipotent). </p>
									<p>In  the <em>C. elegans </em>gonad, the undifferentiated cells in the distal gonad (<a href="../hermaphrodite/germ line/Images/germfig3A_Dleg.htm" target="_blank">mitotic zone</a>) fit this  definition best. Although their  immediate daughters only go on to become spermatocytes or oocytes or to undergo  programmed cell death, their distant progeny (after mating) are totipotent.  They can undergo many divisions and are nearly immortal.</p>
								<p>Among <em>C. elegans</em> somatic cells, the term is  most often used to refer to (differentiated) cells that are capable of  undergoing repeated cell divisions to produce new daughter cells of various  types, while at each division, one daughter retains the initial cell fate. Examples include the <a href="../hermaphrodite/seam cells/Seamframeset.html" target="_blank">seam cells</a>, which can  repeatedly divide while one daughter retains the seam cell fate after each  division. These cells are  not truly totipotent, but produce a limited, predictable set of  daughters, and unlike in higher animals, these cells have a differentiated  phenotype themselves. The P ventral neuroblasts are another example since they also display a stem cell-like division pattern and they remain undifferentiated  into larval life at which point they undergo a fixed number of divisions to produce a  limited range of progeny.</p>

							<td valign=top>Symmetric sisters phenotype</td>
							<td valign=top><span class="style26"></span></td>
							<td valign=top><p>A genetic mutation which causes a failure of the  asymmetric cell division of the <a href="../hermaphrodite/somatic gonad/Images/somaticfig1leg.htm" target="_blank">Z1 and Z4 blast cells</a> in the somatic germline  such that both daughter cells (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/cell/cell.cgi?name=Z1.a;class=Cell" target="_blank">Z1.a</a> and <a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/cell/cell.cgi?name=Z1.p;class=Cell" target="_blank">Z1.p</a>, etc.) are equivalent in size and  adopt the same fates, producing an AC/VU precursor but never a DTC fate. The  resulting gonad fails to extend out &ldquo;arms&rdquo; from the developing germline, and instead produces a single central mass of germ cells near the vulva  (<a href="http://www.wormbase.org/db/misc/paper?name=WBPaper00004521;class=Paper" target="_blank">Miskowski et al., 2001</a>).</p></td>
